## MODULE 2 — RISK MANAGEMENT (STAYING IN THE GAME)

### 2.1 Stop losses: decide your exit before you enter

Before every trade, write three numbers down: **Entry, Stop, Target.** If you can't name the stop, you don't take the trade.

- Put the stop at a price that proves your idea **wrong**, like just under a support level (Module 3), not at a random round number.  
- Once you're in, you can move the stop **up** to lock in profit. You **never** move it down to "give it room." Moving the stop down is how a $10 loss becomes a $100 loss.  
- Most broker apps let you place a stop order right when you buy, so it works even while you're on a delivery.

### 2.2 Risk/reward: risk $10 to make $20 or more

**Risk/reward** compares what you could lose to what you could make.

Example: Entry **$20.00**, stop **$19.00**, target **$22.00**.

- Risk per share: $20 − $19 = **$1.00**  
- Reward per share: $22 − $20 = **$2.00**  
- Ratio: $2 ÷ $1 = **2 to 1** (written 2:1)

So if you risk $10 on that trade (10 shares), you're aiming to make $20.

**House rule: only take trades at 2:1 or better.**

### 2.3 Why that matters: you can be wrong a lot and still win

The win rate you need just to break even:

> **Break-even win rate = 1 ÷ (1 \+ reward/risk)**

| Risk/Reward | Break-even win rate |
| :---- | :---- |
| 1:1 | 1 ÷ 2 = **50%** |
| 2:1 | 1 ÷ 3 = **33.3%** |
| 3:1 | 1 ÷ 4 = **25%** |

At 2:1, you can lose 2 out of every 3 trades and still break even. That's breathing room.

### 2.4 Why win rate alone lies

Someone in a group chat brags, "I win 70% of my trades\!" Sounds great, right? Not until you know how big the wins and losses are.

**Expectancy** = what you make or lose per trade on average, over many trades:

> **Expectancy = (Win % × Average Win) − (Loss % × Average Loss)**

**Trader A, the "70% winner":** wins 70%, average win $10, average loss $30.

- (0.70 × $10) − (0.30 × $30) = $7 − $9 = **−$2 per trade**  
- Over 50 trades: 50 × −$2 = **−$100**. Losing money while "winning most trades."

**Trader B, the "40% winner":** wins 40%, average win $30, average loss $10.

- (0.40 × $30) − (0.60 × $10) = $12 − $6 = **\+$6 per trade**  
- Over 50 trades: 50 × $6 = **\+$300**.

Trader B loses more often and makes more money. **Small losses plus bigger wins beat a high win rate.** Track your expectancy in your journal (Module 5). It's the one number that tells you the truth.

### 2.5 Daily max-loss cutoff: know when to walk away

Bad days make people "revenge trade," meaning they chase losses with bigger, sloppier bets. Stop it before it starts:

- **Daily stop: 3% of your account OR 3 losing trades in a row, whichever comes first.** Then you're done for the day. Close the app and go take orders.  
- $1,000 account → 3% = **$30**. Once you're down $30 for the day, you stop.  
- **Weekly stop: 6%.** Down 6% for the week ($60 on $1,000) means you're done until Monday. Use the weekend for review.

### 2.6 Drawdown math: why big losses are so hard to come back from

When you lose a percentage, getting back to even takes a **bigger** percentage, because you're climbing back with less money.

> **Gain needed to recover = Loss ÷ (1 − Loss)**

| You lose | You need to gain this much just to get back |
| :---- | :---- |
| 10% | 11.1% |
| 20% | 25% |
| 30% | 42.9% |
| **50%** | **100%** |
| 75% | 300% |
| 90% | 900% |

Example: $1,000 drops 50% to $500. To get back to $1,000 you need to make $500 on $500. That's **\+100%**, doubling your money just to get back to where you started.

That's why the 1% rule exists. Small losses are easy to recover. Big ones can take years or never come back.

### 2.7 Other risk rules for beginners

- **No borrowed money.** That means no "margin" (broker loans), no credit cards and no loans from family to trade.  
- **No options or leverage while learning.** These products can multiply losses fast. Learn the basics on plain shares first.  
- **Watch for hidden costs.** "Commission-free" doesn't mean free. There's still a gap between the buy and sell price (called the "spread"), and it's wider on cheap, thinly traded stocks.  
- **Small-account day-trading rules.** Brokers have rules that limit how often small accounts can buy and sell the same stock in one day, and those rules have been under review lately. Check your broker's current rules before you ever day trade. Holding trades for a few days ("swing trading") avoids most of this and fits gig schedules better anyway.
